Branching Networks: From a Random Neighbor to a Giant Component

Created2026-08-11|Updated2026-11-12|Complex Systems
|Word Count:4.6k

Complex Systems · 7/11 · Series index · Notation

Suppose half the people in a town have one connection each and the other half have three. Pair the connections at random. The average person has two contacts. Yet if you follow a randomly chosen connection, you have a three-in-four chance of arriving at someone with three contacts. That bias determines whether a message sent from one person can reach most of the town. In a large town like this, about 81% of the people will belong to a single connected group. We can find that fraction without drawing the network.

Who is at the other end of an edge?

When a network looks like a tree

The configuration model gives each vertex the prescribed number of half-edges, then pairs the half-edges uniformly at random. In the large, sparse, uncorrelated model, a short exploration from a typical starting vertex, or root, resembles a tree. Each unused edge initially leads to a fresh vertex. This is an approximation about a neighborhood of fixed radius; the whole graph can still contain many cycles.

The orange edge is the one used to reach the highlighted vertex. Counting its degree gives three; counting the two green edges gives its excess degree. Across the whole drawing the first shell contains three vertices and the second shell contains four. On a network with triangles, two such branches could meet and the shell would be smaller.

There is a quantitative check on this picture. Take two neighbors of the root with aaa and bbb unused half-edges respectively. There are ababab ways to pair one half-edge from each neighbor, out of roughly 2M2M2M possible partners per half-edge. When these probabilities are small, the chance that the pair closes a triangle is about ab/(2M)ab/(2M)ab/(2M). The neighbors' excess degrees are approximately independent and each has mean BBB. Averaging gives the expected clustering of this random neighborhood:

C ≃ B22M=(m2−c)2nc3,C\ \simeq\ \frac{B^2}{2M} =\frac{(m_2-c)^2}{n c^3}, C ≃ 2MB2​=nc3(m2​−c)2​,

where CCC is the probability that two neighbors are adjacent. At fixed ccc and finite m2m_2m2​, this tends to zero. More generally, among a fixed number of exposed vertices with bounded degrees, the chance of any extra edge closing a loop is of order 1/n1/n1/n. A heavy tail, very large hubs or imposed triangles can invalidate this estimate; a numerical prediction C>1C>1C>1 means the approximation has broken down. In an Erdős–Rényi graph each pair is linked independently with probability ppp, so the triangle-closing probability is exactly ppp.

The fraction of the network one vertex can reach

A giant component contains a nonzero fraction SSS of all vertices as nnn grows. Every finite graph has a largest component, but its fraction can shrink to zero. In a homogeneous random network there is normally at most one giant: two extensive pieces would have many chances to meet. For instance, take two fixed disjoint groups occupying fractions aaa and bbb of the vertices in a sparse Erdős–Rényi graph. The probability that none of their abn2abn^2abn2 potential cross-links exists is approximately (1−c/n)abn2(1-c/n)^{abn^2}(1−c/n)abn2, exponentially small in nnn. This explains the role of random mixing; networks with deliberately isolated populations need not behave this way.
