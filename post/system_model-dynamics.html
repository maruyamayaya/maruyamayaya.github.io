Fixed Points, Bifurcations, and the Growth of Small Errors

发表于2026-08-11|更新于2026-11-12|复杂系统
|总字数:4.2k

Complex Systems · 10/11 · Series index · Notation

Start with any number between zero and one. Square it, and keep squaring. Starting at 111 gives 111 forever. Starting at 0.990.990.99 gives 0.98010.98010.9801, then about 0.96060.96060.9606, and eventually a number very close to zero. Both 000 and 111 solve the equation “the next value equals the present value.” Only one attracts the nearby starting values in this interval. That distinction matters whenever we solve a model by iterating it.

The rule determines the meaning of equilibrium

A state records the quantities that change. A parameter selects the rule under which they change. Let xxx be a scalar state, rrr a parameter held fixed during one run, and FFF a function. Two superficially similar models ask different questions:

The star marks an equilibrium. Starting exactly there keeps the state there. To test stability, start slightly away. A Lyapunov-stable equilibrium keeps sufficiently small displacements small; an asymptotically stable equilibrium also brings them back to zero. The set of initial states drawn to an attractor is its basin of attraction. An attractor can be a periodic orbit rather than a point: the system approaches a repeating motion without ever coming to rest.

For a scalar map, draw the curve y=F(x;r)y=F(x;r)y=F(x;r) and the diagonal y=xy=xy=x, where yyy is the vertical plotting coordinate. Their intersections are fixed points. From the initial value on the horizontal axis, go vertically to the curve to read the next value, then horizontally to the diagonal to reuse that value as the next input. Repeating these two moves gives a cobweb diagram. A staircase that closes in on an intersection depicts attraction; one that crosses back and forth can still converge if each crossing is smaller.

There is a network interpretation. Let uuu be the probability that following an edge fails to reach the giant component, and let g1g_1g1​ be the probability-generating function for the number of other edges at the far endpoint. A branching factor above one means the all-failure solution repels a small success probability. The branching calculation and this stability test measure the same growth.

How equilibrium branches appear, disappear, and remember

A bifurcation occurs when changing a parameter changes the qualitative long-term motion. Draw the control parameter horizontally and the equilibrium value vertically. A single vertical slice then shows every equilibrium at that setting. Solid and dashed lines below denote attracting and unstable branches, respectively. The point where an eigenvalue reaches its stability boundary is a candidate bifurcation; checking both sides reveals what actually changes.

Three scalar flows illustrate the distinction. All variables and parameters in these examples are scaled to be dimensionless.

A similar distinction is observable in traffic. Two snapshots with the same fraction of congested road segments can have very different sizes of the largest connected region of passable roads. The proportion of roads still open does not specify which routes remain connected. Different outward and return paths in such a plot are evidence of history dependence; the cubic model explains one mechanism that can produce it.

Losing a fixed point can produce a rhythm

Consider an oscillator with position xxx, velocity yyy, and parameter rrr controlling damping or energy input:

x˙=y,y˙=r(1−x2)y−x.\dot x=y,\qquad \dot y=r(1-x^2)y-x . x˙=y,y˙​=r(1−x2)y−x.

Both rates vanish only at the origin. There the Jacobian and its characteristic equation are

J=(01−1r),λ2−rλ+1=0,λ±=r±r2−42.J=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&r\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \lambda^2-r\lambda+1=0,\qquad \lambda_\pm=\frac{r\pm\sqrt{r^2-4}}{2}. J=(0−1​1r​),λ2−rλ+1=0,λ±​=2r±r2−4​​.

Near r=0r=0r=0 the conjugate eigenvalues have real part r/2r/2r/2. A nearby trajectory spirals inward for negative rrr and outward for positive rrr: the local signature of a Hopf-type loss of stability. The Jacobian only describes the neighborhood of the origin. To see what limits the outward motion, define the energy-like quantity E=(x2+y2)/2E=(x^2+y^2)/2E=(x2+y2)/2. Then

E˙=xx˙+yy˙=r(1−x2)y2.\dot E=x\dot x+y\dot y=r(1-x^2)y^2 . E˙=xx˙+yy˙​=r(1−x2)y2.

For r>0r>0r>0, the motion gains energy where ∣x∣<1|x|<1∣x∣<1 and loses it where ∣x∣>1|x|>1∣x∣>1. In this Van der Pol oscillator the two effects sustain a stable limit cycle, an isolated closed orbit approached by nearby trajectories. At r=0r=0r=0, in contrast, EEE is constant: every initial radius gives its own circle and no circle attracts its neighbors. This center is degenerate; the familiar square-root amplitude rule of a generic supercritical Hopf bifurcation does not apply to this parameterization.

The approach can be deceptively slow. Write a continuous eigenvalue as λ=α+iω\lambda=\alpha+\mathrm{i}\omegaλ=α+iω, where α\alphaα is the growth rate and ω\omegaω the angular frequency. The displacement oscillates while its envelope varies as eαte^{\alpha t}eαt. On the stable side its relaxation time, the time to shrink by a factor of eee, is τ=1/∣α∣\tau=1/|\alpha|τ=1/∣α∣. As α\alphaα approaches zero from below, the trajectory can complete many turns before its radius noticeably changes. This is critical slowing down. For a discrete multiplier of magnitude 0<∣a∣<10<|a|<10<∣a∣<1, the corresponding number of steps is −1/ln⁡∣a∣-1/\ln|a|−1/ln∣a∣, since ∣a∣n=enln⁡∣a∣|a|^n=e^{n\ln|a|}∣a∣n=enln∣a∣. An alternating signal that decays slowly can easily be mistaken for a persistent cycle.

One update rule, from a fixed point to a two-cycle

Return to discrete time and let F(x;r)=x+r−x2F(x;r)=x+r-x^2F(x;r)=x+r−x2. Its fixed points are the same two roots ±r\pm\sqrt r±r​ as for the continuous saddle-node. The positive root now has multiplier 1−2r1-2\sqrt r1−2r​: it attracts exponentially for 0<r<10<r<10<r<1. At r=1r=1r=1 this multiplier reaches −1-1−1. A displacement changes sign each step, and as rrr rises just beyond one the system visits two distinct values in alternation. This is period doubling.

To find the two values, look for states that return after two steps. Write F∘2F^{\circ2}F∘2 for applying FFF twice at the same rrr. Substitution and factorization give

F∘2(x)−x=−(x2−r)(x2−2x+2−r).F^{\circ2}(x)-x=-(x^2-r)(x^2-2x+2-r). F∘2(x)−x=−(x2−r)(x2−2x+2−r).

To test attraction, follow a small displacement all the way back:

μ2=F′(b)F′(a)=(1−2b)(1−2a)=5−4r.\mu_2=F'(b)F'(a)=(1-2b)(1-2a)=5-4r . μ2​=F′(b)F′(a)=(1−2b)(1−2a)=5−4r.

Read the upper plot vertically. Each horizontal position holds rrr fixed; the dots are successive states after discarding the early transient. One height means a fixed point, two heights a two-cycle, and four heights a four-cycle. Dense bands can alternate with periodic windows. A time series would use step number on the horizontal axis and show only one value of rrr. Too few discarded steps thicken the branches precisely where critical slowing makes convergence slowest.

What the Lyapunov exponent measures

On the lower plot, the vertical quantity is the Lyapunov exponent Λ\LambdaΛ: the mean exponential growth rate of an infinitesimal displacement along the orbit being followed. A derivative at a fixed point measures one repeated stretch. On a moving orbit each step stretches by a different factor. Write xjx_jxj​ for the state just before update jjj, and δ0\delta_0δ0​ for the initial displacement. The chain rule gives

∣δn∣∣δ0∣ ≃ ∣(F∘n)′(x0)∣=∏j=0n−1∣F′(xj)∣,Λ=lim⁡n→∞1n∑j=0n−1ln⁡∣F′(xj)∣.\frac{|\delta_n|}{|\delta_0|} \ \simeq\ \left|(F^{\circ n})'(x_0)\right| =\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}|F'(x_j)|,\qquad \Lambda=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\ln|F'(x_j)|. ∣δ0​∣∣δn​∣​ ≃ ∣(F∘n)′(x0​)∣=j=0∏n−1​∣F′(xj​)∣,Λ=n→∞lim​n1​j=0∑n−1​ln∣F′(xj​)∣.

Here ln⁡\lnln is the natural logarithm and the limit is assumed to exist. Take the infinitesimal-displacement limit first to obtain the derivative; then take the long-time average. Taking logarithms turns successive magnifications into additions. While a disturbance is small enough for the linear approximation, its typical size scales as ∣δn∣≈∣δ0∣eΛn|\delta_n|\approx|\delta_0|e^{\Lambda n}∣δn​∣≈∣δ0​∣eΛn. For example, Λ=ln⁡2\Lambda=\ln 2Λ=ln2 means a tiny initial uncertainty typically doubles per step. A negative exponent means net exponential contraction; a positive one means exponential sensitivity. Zero means neither exponential growth nor decay on average. It can mark a bifurcation and does not, by itself, imply chaos. In a bounded chaotic system the distance between two actual trajectories eventually saturates; the exponent describes the growth of an infinitesimal perturbation, not a finite distance for all time. In the examples here, chaos combines deterministic rules, bounded aperiodic motion, and sustained sensitivity to initial conditions along typical trajectories. An unbounded map that simply doubles every value also has a positive exponent.

On a periodic orbit of period ppp (the number of updates for one return), the same ppp slopes repeat. If μp\mu_pμp​ denotes their product, then

Λ=ln⁡∣μp∣p.\Lambda=\frac{\ln|\mu_p|}{p}. Λ=pln∣μp​∣​.

The division converts a growth rate per return to one per step. A stable periodic branch lies below zero on the exponent plot. At a doubling, the old orbit's return multiplier is −1-1−1, so its exponent is zero; measuring over twice as many steps gives (−1)2=+1(-1)^2=+1(−1)2=+1 on the same critical orbit. After the doubling a different periodic orbit can have a negative exponent again. At a superstable parameter one slope vanishes and the mathematical exponent is −∞-\infty−∞; the lower plot omits values below −2.5-2.5−2.5, so the deepest troughs run off its scale. In the quadratic map the fixed point is superstable at r=1/4r=1/4r=1/4, and the two-cycle at 5/45/45/4.

For a vector map, the Jacobian changes with position. Write JjJ_jJj​ for its value at step jjj and Mn=Jn−1⋯J1J0M_n=J_{n-1}\cdots J_1J_0Mn​=Jn−1​⋯J1​J0​ for the total linear transformation. The earliest update acts first and therefore sits on the right. The greatest stretch of a unit displacement is the matrix norm ∥Mn∥2\|M_n\|_2∥Mn​∥2​, equal to the largest singular value. The largest exponent is the long-time growth rate lim sup⁡n→∞n−1ln⁡∥Mn∥2\limsup_{n\to\infty} n^{-1}\ln\|M_n\|_2limsupn→∞​n−1ln∥Mn​∥2​, where the upper limit allows for fluctuations. One cannot generally multiply the largest eigenvalues of the individual matrices: the direction most stretched on one step need not be the direction most stretched on the next. At a fixed point the matrix is constant, and the result reduces to the logarithm of the largest eigenvalue modulus.

When time is continuous: the Lorenz attractor

The Lorenz system describes three coupled state coordinates x,y,zx,y,zx,y,z. Positive parameters sss and bbb set the rate at which xxx adjusts toward yyy and the damping of zzz, respectively; rrr controls the strength of the drive in the yyy equation:

x˙=s(y−x),y˙=rx−y−xz,z˙=xy−bz.\dot x=s(y-x),\qquad \dot y=rx-y-xz,\qquad \dot z=xy-bz . x˙=s(y−x),y˙​=rx−y−xz,z˙=xy−bz.

At equilibrium y=xy=xy=x and z=x2/bz=x^2/bz=x2/b, so x(r−1−x2/b)=0x(r-1-x^2/b)=0x(r−1−x2/b)=0. The origin always exists. For r>1r>1r>1 there are two further equilibria with x=y=±b(r−1)x=y=\pm\sqrt{b(r-1)}x=y=±b(r−1)​ and z=r−1z=r-1z=r−1. At the origin the zzz direction has eigenvalue −b-b−b. The other two eigenvalues solve λ2+(s+1)λ+s(1−r)=0\lambda^2+(s+1)\lambda+s(1-r)=0λ2+(s+1)λ+s(1−r)=0. For r<1r<1r<1 their sum is negative and their product positive, so both have negative real part. For r>1r>1r>1 their product is negative, leaving one growing mode. The origin becomes a saddle. For example, at s=10s=10s=10, r=30r=30r=30, b=3b=3b=3, a typical trajectory circles one lobe, switches to the other, and continues in a bounded but nonperiodic pattern. A single trajectory is a curve; the attractor traced out in the long-time limit has a folded, fractal structure of dimension between two and three. There is no single fractal dimension valid for every choice of parameters.

A smooth autonomous flow assigns only one velocity to each state. With unique solutions, its paths cannot cross. In the plane, the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem constrains bounded limit sets (under its usual hypotheses, including finitely many equilibria), ruling out a Lorenz-type chaotic attractor. A third coordinate lets a trajectory pass above another strand that appears to cross it in projection. A one-dimensional discrete map already supplied a route to chaos: a continuous trajectory constrained to a plane and a map sampled step by step obey different geometric rules.

This distinction matters outside physics, too. A recurrent neural network iterates a hidden state. To ask whether that state settles, cycles, or responds sensitively to its starting value, hold the weights and input protocol fixed. Training changes the weights, so a plot against training epoch scans different update rules. Reorganization of the hidden states near a minimum of test loss can flag a change worth investigating. It does not establish that a particular bifurcation is what improved prediction. The first modelling decision is still the same one we made when squaring a number: identify exactly what updates, and what stays fixed.
