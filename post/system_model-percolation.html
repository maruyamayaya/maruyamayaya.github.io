Percolation and Epidemics: How Far Can One Seed Reach?

发表于2026-08-11|更新于2026-11-12|复杂系统
|总字数:4k

Complex Systems · 8/11 · Series index · Notation

Imagine a rail network where every station has three neighbors. A storm closes a quarter of the stations at random. Another storm leaves every station open but closes a quarter of the tracks. In which case can more people still travel across the network? What if the tracks are social ties, and only three in four ties pass on a message?

All three questions concern percolation: which parts of a network remain reachable after some of its nodes or edges become unavailable? A giant cluster is a connected set containing a nonzero fraction of the original nodes as the network grows. A trip need not reach every station to reach the giant; it only needs one way in.

Follow one connection

Take a large, undirected random network with no degree correlations and few short loops. Near a typical node, different outward branches can then be treated as independent. Let KKK be the degree of a uniformly chosen node, kkk a possible degree, pk=Pr⁡(K=k)p_k=\Pr(K=k)pk​=Pr(K=k) its probability, and c=⟨K⟩>0c=\langle K\rangle>0c=⟨K⟩>0 the mean degree. When does a route across the network appear?

Why hubs change the threshold

In a sparse Erdős–Rényi network the degree distribution is Poisson, with g0(z)=g1(z)=ec(z−1)g_0(z)=g_1(z)=e^{c(z-1)}g0​(z)=g1​(z)=ec(z−1). Then B=cB=cB=c, so ϕc=1/c\phi_c=1/cϕc​=1/c when c>1c>1c>1. The tail of the degree moment of order mmm behaves like ∑kkm−α\sum_k k^{m-\alpha}∑k​km−α, which converges only for α>m+1\alpha>m+1α>m+1. A surviving hub matters twice: it is easy to encounter along an edge and offers many routes onward. Actual finite networks have a degree cutoff, and targeting hubs deliberately violates the assumption of degree-independent removal.

What makes a contact usable?

Does a giant cluster guarantee a large outbreak?

Suppose every undirected contact is independently marked transmissible with probability TTT. From one initial seed, transmission can then reach precisely the nodes in its open-edge component: each reached node has an open path back to the seed, and propagation along any open path eventually reaches its far end. This is bond percolation with ϕ=T\phi=Tϕ=T. It describes a final set, not the times of infection.

In the three-neighbor example at T=3/4T=3/4T=3/4, the giant contains a fraction 26/2726/2726/27 of the population. A seed chosen uniformly at random lands in it with probability 26/2726/2726/27. Conditional on landing there, the outbreak reaches about (26/27)N(26/27)N(26/27)N people; the remaining seeds reach only finite clusters. Averaged over all seeds, the limiting fraction infected is therefore (26/27)2(26/27)^2(26/27)2. In a particular finite cluster of size sss, there are sss distinct choices of seed, which is why the small-outbreak probabilities are the node-weighted snssn_ssns​.

Independent marking is a real modelling assumption. In a temporal SIR process, one person's outward contacts may all share the same random recovery time LLL. A long infectious period makes several transmissions more likely together. The single-edge value TTT and the early branching mean TBTBTB still make sense under the assumptions above, but the probability of a large outbreak need not equal the giant fraction predicted by independent undirected bonds. The equality holds for the independent-bond model.

The same distinction applies to a forwarded message. An account that has never forwarded it is susceptible; a visible repost can prompt new reposts; once it loses visibility it is removed from further transmission. A popular account offers many first opportunities. The number of accounts that actually repost depends on what happens beyond that first hop: who the contacts lead to, whether they relay the message in time, and how often their audiences overlap.
