The Central Limit Theorem: Why Sums of Many Random Contributions Are Approximately Normal

A falling-ball apparatus illustrates a random walk: at each level, a ball moves randomly to the left or right, and its final position is the sum of many random displacements. The central limit theorem explains why suitably standardized sums often approach a normal distribution.

Distinguishing Random Variables, Distributions, and Moments

A random variable XXX assigns a numerical value to the outcome of a random experiment; xxx denotes one of its possible values. A continuous random variable is described by a probability density function (PDF), pX(x)p_X(x)pX​(x):

pX(x)≥0,∫−∞∞pX(x) dx=1,P(a≤X≤b)=∫abpX(x) dx.\begin{aligned} p_X(x)&\geq 0,\\ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_X(x)\,dx&=1,\\ P(a\leq X\leq b)&=\int_a^b p_X(x)\,dx. \end{aligned} pX​(x)∫−∞∞​pX​(x)dxP(a≤X≤b)​≥0,=1,=∫ab​pX​(x)dx.​

The height of a density is not the probability of that exact value; a continuous distribution has P(X=x)=0P(X=x)=0P(X=x)=0. A discrete random variable instead uses a probability mass function (PMF), pX(x)=P(X=x)p_X(x)=P(X=x)pX​(x)=P(X=x), and integration is replaced by summation over all possible values. For example, ∑xpX(x)=1\sum_xp_X(x)=1∑x​pX​(x)=1.

Mean, Variance, and Raw Moments

The mean describes location, the variance measures squared fluctuations around the mean, and the standard deviation σ\sigmaσ has the same units as XXX:

μ=E[X]=∫−∞∞xpX(x) dx,σ2=Var⁡(X)=E[(X−μ)2]=E[X2]−(E[X])2.\begin{aligned} \mu&=E[X]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x p_X(x)\,dx,\\ \sigma^2&=\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[(X-\mu)^2]\\ &=E[X^2]-(E[X])^2. \end{aligned} μσ2​=E[X]=∫−∞∞​xpX​(x)dx,=Var(X)=E[(X−μ)2]=E[X2]−(E[X])2.​

The kkkth raw moment is mk=E[Xk]m_k=E[X^k]mk​=E[Xk]. Therefore,

m0=1,μ=m1,σ2=m2−m12.m_0=1,\qquad \mu=m_1,\qquad \sigma^2=m_2-m_1^2. m0​=1,μ=m1​,σ2=m2​−m12​.

General and Standard Normal Distributions

A general normal, or Gaussian, distribution is written as X∼N(μ,σ2)X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)X∼N(μ,σ2). Its second parameter is the variance:

pX(x)=1σ2πexp⁡ ⁣[−(x−μ)22σ2],σ>0.p_X(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\!\left[-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right],\qquad \sigma>0. pX​(x)=σ2π​1​exp[−2σ2(x−μ)2​],σ>0.

The Precise CLT Statement and Its Assumptions

Let X1,…,XnX_1,\ldots,X_nX1​,…,Xn​ be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.), with

E[Xi]=μ finite,0<Var⁡(Xi)=σ2<∞.E[X_i]=\mu\ \text{finite},\qquad 0<\operatorname{Var}(X_i)=\sigma^2<\infty. E[Xi​]=μ finite,0<Var(Xi​)=σ2<∞.

“Identically distributed” means that all variables follow the same probability law; it does not mean that every observed value is identical. Independence means that the joint probability law factorizes: learning outcomes for any subset does not change the joint distribution of the remaining variables. Define the sum and sample mean as

Sn=∑i=1nXi,X‾n=Snn.S_n=\sum_{i=1}^nX_i,\qquad \overline X_n=\frac{S_n}{n}. Sn​=i=1∑n​Xi​,Xn​=nSn​​.

The central limit theorem (CLT) states that the standardized sum converges in distribution:

Zn=Sn−nμσn=n(X‾n−μ)σ,Zn→dN(0,1),n→∞.\boxed{ \begin{aligned} Z_n&=\frac{S_n-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n} =\frac{\sqrt n(\overline X_n-\mu)}{\sigma},\\ Z_n&\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal N(0,1),\qquad n\to\infty. \end{aligned} } Zn​Zn​​=σn​Sn​−nμ​=σn​(Xn​−μ)​,d​N(0,1),n→∞.​​

Here, convergence in distribution means that P(Zn≤z)→Φ(z)P(Z_n\leq z)\to\Phi(z)P(Zn​≤z)→Φ(z) for every fixed real number zzz. The original variables XiX_iXi​ may be discrete or non-normal. The theorem does not assert exact normality at finite nnn, nor does it guarantee pointwise convergence of arbitrary probability densities.

Why Standardization Uses the Square Root of nnn

The scaling of means and variances explains the appearance of n\sqrt nn​:

E[Sn]=nμ,Var⁡(Sn)=nσ2,E[X‾n]=μ,Var⁡(X‾n)=σ2n.\begin{aligned} E[S_n]&=n\mu,\\ \operatorname{Var}(S_n)&=n\sigma^2,\\ E[\overline X_n]&=\mu,\\ \operatorname{Var}(\overline X_n)&=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}. \end{aligned} E[Sn​]Var(Sn​)E[Xn​]Var(Xn​)​=nμ,=nσ2,=μ,=nσ2​.​

Independence makes the cross-covariances zero. The sum has standard deviation σn\sigma\sqrt nσn​, whereas the mean has standard deviation σ/n\sigma/\sqrt nσ/n​. For large nnn, we therefore often write the approximations

Sn≈N(nμ,nσ2),X‾n≈N ⁣(μ,σ2n).\begin{aligned} S_n&\approx\mathcal N(n\mu,n\sigma^2),\\ \overline X_n&\approx\mathcal N\!\left(\mu,\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right). \end{aligned} Sn​Xn​​≈N(nμ,nσ2),≈N(μ,nσ2​).​

When μ=0,σ2=1\mu=0,\sigma^2=1μ=0,σ2=1, Zn=n X‾n=Sn/nZ_n=\sqrt n\,\overline X_n=S_n/\sqrt nZn​=n​Xn​=Sn​/n​; the square root covers only nnn. The fluctuations of the unstandardized mean shrink to zero as it approaches the constant μ\muμ; we must not write X‾n→N(0,1)\overline X_n\to\mathcal N(0,1)Xn​→N(0,1). A density approximation must also include the correct normalization factor:

pX‾n(x)≈nσ2πexp⁡ ⁣[−n(x−μ)22σ2].p_{\overline X_n}(x)\approx\frac{\sqrt n}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\!\left[-\frac{n(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right]. pXn​​(x)≈σ2π​n​​exp[−2σ2n(x−μ)2​].

Why the CLT Is Useful, and Why Its Assumptions Matter

Wealth, stock prices, earthquakes, and supermarket sales are influenced by many factors, which can motivate a normal model as an initial hypothesis. Before applying this reasoning, check whether the contributions add, whether they are independent, and whether the relevant distributional and finite-variance assumptions hold. The examples themselves do not prove that these quantities are normally distributed. Correlated interactions, multiplicative growth, or very heavy tails can invalidate this simple argument.

The version used here assumes i.i.d. variables with finite variance. Having equal means and variances alone does not imply identical distributions. More general CLTs can relax assumptions, but require additional theorems. There is also no universal threshold such as “n≥30n\geq30n≥30” that guarantees a reliable approximation for every distribution; skewness and heavy tails affect the rate of convergence.

Moment-Generating Functions: Encoding Moments in One Function

The moment-generating function (MGF) is

MX(t)=E[etX]=∫−∞∞etxpX(x) dx.M_X(t)=E[e^{tX}] =\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{tx}p_X(x)\,dx. MX​(t)=E[etX]=∫−∞∞​etxpX​(x)dx.

Expand etXe^{tX}etX around t=0t=0t=0. When summation and expectation can be interchanged,

etX=1+tX+t2X22!+t3X33!+⋯ ,MX(t)=1+m1t+m2t22!+m3t33!+⋯ .\begin{aligned} e^{tX}&=1+tX+\frac{t^2X^2}{2!}+\frac{t^3X^3}{3!}+\cdots,\\ M_X(t)&=1+m_1t+\frac{m_2t^2}{2!}+\frac{m_3t^3}{3!}+\cdots. \end{aligned} etXMX​(t)​=1+tX+2!t2X2​+3!t3X3​+⋯,=1+m1​t+2!m2​t2​+3!m3​t3​+⋯.​

Consequently,

MX(k)(0)=mk=E[Xk].\boxed{M_X^{(k)}(0)=m_k=E[X^k].} MX(k)​(0)=mk​=E[Xk].​

The notation MX(k)M_X^{(k)}MX(k)​ denotes the kkkth derivative, not the kkkth power of MXM_XMX​. Differentiate with respect to ttt first, then set t=0t=0t=0. Substituting t=0t=0t=0 directly into the MGF gives only MX(0)=1M_X(0)=1MX​(0)=1.

Not every distribution has such an MGF; even the existence of all raw moments does not automatically guarantee it. Translation, Scaling, and Independent Sums

The proof below uses two rules:

MaX+b(t)=ebtMX(at),MX+Y(t)=MX(t)MY(t)(X,Y independent).\begin{aligned} M_{aX+b}(t)&=e^{bt}M_X(at),\\ M_{X+Y}(t)&=M_X(t)M_Y(t)\qquad (X,Y\ \text{independent}). \end{aligned} MaX+b​(t)MX+Y​(t)​=ebtMX​(at),=MX​(t)MY​(t)(X,Y independent).​

The first follows from et(aX+b)=ebteatXe^{t(aX+b)}=e^{bt}e^{atX}et(aX+b)=ebteatX. The second uses independence to obtain E[etXetY]=E[etX]E[etY]E[e^{tX}e^{tY}]=E[e^{tX}]E[e^{tY}]E[etXetY]=E[etX]E[etY]. Being merely uncorrelated is generally insufficient for the second rule.

The MGF Proof of the CLT: Where Each Assumption Is Used

Consider the simplified case E[Xi]=0E[X_i]=0E[Xi​]=0, E[Xi2]=1E[X_i^2]=1E[Xi2​]=1, and assumes that the MGF of an individual XiX_iXi​ is finite near 000. The goal is to show that the MGF of Zn=Sn/nZ_n=S_n/\sqrt nZn​=Sn​/n​ converges to et2/2e^{t^2/2}et2/2.

MZn(t)=∏i=1nE[etXi/n]=[MX ⁣(tn)]n.\begin{aligned} M_{Z_n}(t)&=\prod_{i=1}^n E[e^{tX_i/\sqrt n}]\\ &=\left[M_X\!\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt n}\right)\right]^n. \end{aligned} MZn​​(t)​=i=1∏n​E[etXi​/n​]=[MX​(n​t​)]n.​

Repeat the argument for Yi=(Xi−μ)/σY_i=(X_i-\mu)/\sigmaYi​=(Xi​−μ)/σ. Since E[Yi]=0E[Y_i]=0E[Yi​]=0 and Var⁡(Yi)=1\operatorname{Var}(Y_i)=1Var(Yi​)=1,

Random Walks: From One Step to the Endpoint Distribution

Consider a walk in which each step is +1+1+1 to the right or −1-1−1 to the left, with probability 1/21/21/2 each. Steps are independent, and the position after nnn steps is Sn=X1+⋯+XnS_n=X_1+\cdots+X_nSn​=X1​+⋯+Xn​. Repeated left or right deflections in the falling-ball apparatus provide an intuitive analogy.

We can also see this directly: an odd power of XXX equals XXX, while an even power is always 111. A single step therefore has mean 000 and variance 111. Its distribution is not normal: its fourth moment is 111, whereas the fourth moment of a standard normal variable is 333.

E[Sn]=0,E[Sn2]=n,E[Sn3]=0,E[Sn4]=3n2−2n.\begin{aligned} E[S_n]&=0,\\ E[S_n^2]&=n,\\ E[S_n^3]&=0,\\ E[S_n^4]&=3n^2-2n. \end{aligned} E[Sn​]E[Sn2​]E[Sn3​]E[Sn4​]​=0,=n,=0,=3n2−2n.​

  • All three values of nnn are odd, and possible endpoints are separated by 222. Histogram bins of width 222, centered on the allowed endpoints, are appropriate. Normalize the histogram as a density when overlaying a PDF. If the bars instead show PMF values, compare them with the normal distribution’s probability areas over the corresponding intervals, rather than directly with density heights.
  • The theoretical parameters are μ=0,σ=n\mu=0,\sigma=\sqrt nμ=0,σ=n​. When fitting the simulated sample, report the sample mean and fitted standard deviation; these will generally differ slightly because of sampling fluctuations.
  • As nnn increases, the overall profile of the standardized endpoints generally becomes closer to a bell shape. At finite nnn, the true distribution remains discrete, and a finite set of 100010001000 simulations retains sampling noise.

Question 4: Why Having Many Terms Is Not Enough

Suppose X1=⋯=Xn=YX_1=\cdots=X_n=YX1​=⋯=Xn​=Y, where E[Y]=0E[Y]=0E[Y]=0 and Var⁡(Y)=1\operatorname{Var}(Y)=1Var(Y)=1. Can the ordinary i.i.d. CLT establish Sn/n→N(0,1)S_n/\sqrt n\to\mathcal N(0,1)Sn​/n​→N(0,1)?

Solution: No. These variables are perfectly correlated, and Sn=nYS_n=nYSn​=nY. Identical distributions do not imply independence, and the covariance terms cannot be dropped.
